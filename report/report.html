Summary

We tried to predict how fast traffic will be moving at each of 207 highway sensors in Los Angeles, 15, 30 and 60 minutes from now, using the last hour of sensor readings, the clock, and what the neighbouring sensors are doing.

What we found, in five points:

  1. 8% of the readings are broken, not slow. A reading of exactly 0 mph is a sensor that was offline. We marked those cells "unknown" — nothing deleted, nothing guessed — and the models handle them directly.
  2. Traffic follows the clock. Weekdays have two rush hours; at 17:00, 35% of sensors are jammed. A sensor's speed is 84% correlated with its own speed one hour earlier, so "what it usually does at this time of week" and "what it did in the last hour" are both strong clues.
  3. Neighbours matter, and physics says why. A jam grows backwards, like a queue, at 15–20 km/h (the LWR model). Sensors under 1 km apart have a speed correlation of 0.85; 10 km apart, 0.19. We turned this into eight neighbour features.
  4. Our best model works. XGBoost with neighbour features has an average error of 2.87 / 3.32 / 3.78 mph at 15 / 30 / 60 minutes, against 5.33 mph for "the speed stays the same" and 4.19 mph for "the usual speed" at 60 minutes. A published deep graph model (DCRNN) reaches 3.60 at 60 minutes, so a classical model gets close.
  5. Neighbour information helps most in rush hour and least at 60 minutes. Adding the eight neighbour features lowers the error by 4.1 / 3.3 / 1.8%, three times more in the peaks than off-peak. One hop of neighbours only "sees" 5–15 minutes ahead; that fading is exactly the gap a multi-hop graph network should close in Phase 2.

1. Why we chose this problem

  • It matters in real life. A traffic-signal controller or ramp meter has to act before the jam arrives. Knowing the speed now is not enough; it needs the next hour. Phase 1 builds that forecast; Phase 2 will add the control part, in simulation only.
  • The data is real and public. We used METR-LA (Li et al., 2018): 207 loop detectors on Los Angeles County highways, a reading every five minutes from 1 March to 27 June 2012, plus a road map that says which sensors are neighbours. It is the standard benchmark, so our numbers can be compared with published ones.
  • It is a real machine-learning challenge. The data is a time series, so a random train/test split would cheat. Eight percent of readings are broken. Errors concentrate in rush hour, where the forecast matters most. And the project brief asks a specific question: how much does a sensor gain from looking at its neighbours?

Traffic has been modelled as a fluid since the 1950s: Lighthill and Whitham (1955) and Richards (1956) showed that cars are conserved along a road and that disturbances travel as waves — forwards when the road is empty, backwards when it is congested. Short-term forecasting has a long history of statistical models (Vlahogianni et al., 2014), with two simple predictors — "the usual speed" and "the speed stays the same" — remaining the yardsticks every method is judged against. Tree ensembles such as gradient boosting beat linear models for travel-time prediction (Zhang & Haghani, 2015). The decisive step in recent years was to treat the sensors as a graph: DCRNN (Li et al., 2018) released the METR-LA benchmark we use, and graph attention models (Veličković et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2019) let the model learn how much to listen to each neighbour. On testing, Bergmeir and Benítez (2012) show that ordinary cross-validation is invalid for time series and that one must split by time. Phase 1 uses only classical models; its job is to build the baseline and the honest test bench that the Phase 2 graph model will be compared against.

2. The data

What the dataset looks like

METR-LA is one big table: 34,272 rows (one per five-minute slot over 119 days) and 207 columns (one per sensor). Each cell is the average speed in miles per hour. There are no missing rows: every five-minute slot from 1 March to 27 June 2012 is present.

It comes with a road map: where each sensor is, the driving distance between sensor pairs, and from those an adjacency matrix that says how strongly each sensor is linked to each neighbour (nearer = stronger; links longer than a cut-off are dropped). We rebuilt this matrix from the raw distance table and checked that it matches the published one exactly. The graph has 1,515 directed links, about 7 neighbours per sensor, and one sensor with no neighbours.

Data quality: the readings of 0 mph

8.1% of all cells contain exactly 0 mph. A highway never averages exactly zero over five minutes, so these are not jams — they are sensors that were switched off. Three things confirm it:

  • The zeros sit between normal readings: a sensor reads 69, then 0, 0, 0, then 67 within 25 minutes.
  • They come in runs: 25,963 runs, median 2 slots, but the longest 1,683 slots (almost six days).
  • On 6.3% of time slots every sensor reads 0 at once — a data-collection failure, not 207 simultaneous jams.

So we relabel every 0 as "unknown". Nothing is deleted: the table keeps all its rows and columns, and the worst sensor (20% unknown) is kept. Nothing is invented either: we do not fill the gaps with guessed values, because the long runs would carry stale readings through rush hours, and guessed answers would corrupt the scoring. The tree models accept unknown inputs directly; an example whose answer is unknown is simply skipped, both in training and in scoring (the same convention as the DCRNN paper).

After this cleaning the speeds have a mean of 58.5 mph and a median of 63.2 mph; 9.0% are below 35 mph, the threshold Caltrans uses for "congested". The distribution has two humps — free flow around 65 mph and a congested mode around 20–30 mph — which is the two-regime picture from traffic physics showing up in the data.

3. How we avoided cheating (data leakage)

"Leakage" means the model sees information it would not have in real life, which makes its score look better than it really is. We took six steps against it:

  1. Split by date, not at random. Two readings five minutes apart are nearly identical. A random split would put near-copies of the test data into training, and the score would look far better than it really is (Bergmeir & Benítez, 2012).
  2. Leave a gap. A one-hour gap at each boundary, so no training example's answer falls inside the validation or test period.
  3. Only look backwards. Every input is built from the past: the sensor's last hour, and the neighbours' speeds now, 5 and 10 minutes ago. The answer is only ever used as the answer.
  4. Compute "the usual speed" from training weeks only. The time-of-week average for each sensor is a strong feature; it is calculated on March–May and then simply looked up for June.
  5. Fit the scaler on train only. The linear model needs standardised inputs; the scaling constants come from training rows.
  6. Use the test set once. All model settings were fixed in advance; the validation weeks only decide when to stop training; the test weeks were scored once, at the end.

Two of our eight automated tests check this directly: that no test sample's input window reaches into the training targets, and that the "usual speed" profile does not change when the test data is altered.

Why the neighbour features: jams grow backwards

Think of a queue at a ticket counter: new people join at the back, so the queue grows backwards. A traffic jam does the same. In the Lighthill–Whitham–Richards (LWR) model, cars are conserved along the road, and speed falls as the road gets denser (Greenshields, 1935). A small disturbance then travels at the wave speed c(ρ) = vf(1 − 2ρ/ρj), where vf is the free-flow speed and ρj the density at which nothing moves. When the road is emptier than half the jam density, c is positive and information moves forward with the cars; when it is more congested, c is negative and information moves backwards, against the cars. The tail of a real jam moves upstream at about 15–20 km/h (Treiber & Kesting, 2013).

Three decisions follow:

  • Whether sensor i will be congested soon depends on its downstream neighbours (a queue growing back towards it) and its upstream neighbours (traffic about to arrive). So we keep separate upstream and downstream averages.
  • Nearer neighbours matter more, so the averages are weighted by the road-graph link strength, and unknown neighbours are left out of the average.
  • A backward wave covers 10–20 km in an hour — more than one hop in our graph. One-hop features should therefore help most at short horizons and run out of reach at 60 minutes. A multi-layer graph network (Phase 2) can look several hops out.

6. The models

We trained five models, from the simplest to the most powerful. Each one answers one question.

ModelWhat it doesThe question it answers
Historical averagePredicts the sensor's usual speed at this time of week. No learning.How far does "the usual" get you?
PersistencePredicts the speed stays what it is now. No learning.How far does "right now" get you?
Ridge regressionGives each input a weight and adds them up (a straight line); a penalty keeps the weights small.Is a simple linear model enough?
XGBoostBuilds many small decision trees one after another, each fixing the last one's mistakes.Do combinations of clues help ("5 pm and speed dropping and the sensor ahead is slow")?
Random ForestBuilds 100 trees on random samples of the data and averages them; one forest predicts all three horizons at once.Is XGBoost's result just one method's quirk?

One model per sensor. Sensors have different personalities, so we train a separate model for each of the 207 sensors (and, for XGBoost, one per sensor per horizon: 621 models, about 12 minutes on two CPU cores).

Settings were chosen in advance from standard practice, not tuned on the test set. XGBoost: tree depth 6, learning rate 0.05, at least 5 examples per leaf, 80% of rows and columns per tree, squared-error loss, at most 600 trees with early stopping — stop when the validation error has not improved for 40 trees. On average only 129 of the 600 trees were kept, so the models are not over-fitting. Random Forest: 100 trees, depth at most 14, at least 5 examples per leaf, half the features considered at each split. Ridge: penalty α = 1, inputs standardised, unknown inputs filled with the sensor's usual speed (the only model that needs filling).

Both tree libraries accept unknown inputs: at every split, the tree learns which way to send a missing value. That is why the masked outages need no filling.

How we score the models

With x the true speed and x̂ the prediction, over all test examples whose true speed is known:

  • MAE (mean absolute error) — the average size of the mistake, in mph. Our headline number: easy to read, robust, and what the literature reports for this dataset.
  • RMSE (root mean squared error) — punishes big misses more. In traffic, the big miss is a jam that was not seen coming.
  • MAPE (mean absolute percentage error) — a percentage, reported because papers do, but it balloons whenever the true speed is small, so it should be read next to MAE.
  • Masked means unknown true speeds are skipped, so an outage never counts as a mistake. Without this, a correct forecast of 67 mph during an outage would be scored as a 67 mph error.
  • Sliced: by horizon (15/30/60 min), rush hour vs off-peak, by hour of day, and per sensor.
  • As a jam detector: call "jam" when the predicted speed is below 35 mph and score it with precision (of the jams we called, how many were real), recall (of the real jams, how many we caught) and F1.

Why not accuracy? For the jam detector, about 90% of answers are "free-flowing". A model that always says "free" gets 89.7% accuracy while catching zero jams. Accuracy hides failure on rare events; MAE, precision and recall do not.

8. Key insights

1. Neighbours help — most in rush hour

Adding eight neighbour numbers to the same XGBoost lowers the error at every horizon, for 147 of 207 sensors, and three times more in the peaks than off-peak. The model's second most-used input is the downstream neighbours. Congestion really does spread between sensors, and a model that looks only at one sensor misses it.

2. The help fades at 60 minutes — and that is the gap for Phase 2

The published graph models beat single-sensor models by margins that grow with the horizon; our hand-built one-hop features help least at 60 minutes. The two facts agree: what matters an hour ahead is several hops away, along paths whose importance changes with the time of day — something a multi-layer, attention-weighted network can represent and a fixed one-hop average cannot. Our measurement is a lower bound on the value of network information, and the horizon at which it fades is where a Graph Attention Network should add the most.

3. Rush hour is the hard part

Error doubles in the peaks and sits almost entirely below 35 mph. The jam detector catches 61% of jams an hour ahead: the ones it misses have not yet reached the sensor's own history. Free-flowing traffic is easy — "the speed stays the same" is already nearly the best forecast there.

4. XGBoost and the Random Forest are a tie

0.01 mph apart on the test set; the forest has the slightly lower RMSE; XGBoost trains three times faster. We did not run a significance test, so we do not claim one is better.

5. The numbers can be trusted

Split by date with a gap; settings fixed in advance; the validation weeks rank the models in exactly the same order as the test weeks; early stopping kept about 130 of 600 trees. One command reruns everything from the raw file to every figure and every results table this report quotes, and eight automated tests check the split, the features and the scoring.

6. Accuracy would have hidden all of this

For the jam detector, "always free" scores 89.7% accuracy. We report MAE in mph, precision and recall instead.

9. Problems we found and fixed in our own pipeline

While checking our work, we found four problems and fixed them before producing the final numbers:

  1. Our first write-up claimed the wrong direction. We expected the neighbour gain to grow with the horizon, as it does for graph networks, and drafted the text that way. The numbers said the opposite (−4.1% at 15 min, −1.8% at 60). We rewrote the claim to match the results and worked out why one-hop features behave this way (Section 5).
  2. The road-graph test was testing nothing. Our first unit test used a toy graph in which all distances were equal, so every link weight fell below the cut-off and the adjacency matrix was empty — the test passed for the wrong reason. We rebuilt it with realistic multi-hop distances; it now checks the real behaviour, and the rebuilt matrix matches the published one exactly.
  3. The data file would not open with the usual tool. The released file is an old-format HDF5 that current pandas cannot read directly. We read it with the lower-level library and documented the file's checksum so anyone can verify they have the same data.
  4. We considered filling the outages and decided against it. Forward-filling the 0 mph gaps looked harmless until we measured the runs: the longest lasts almost six days, so filling would carry a stale reading through many rush hours. We kept the gaps as "unknown" and let the tree models handle them.

10. Limitations

  • One city, four months of 2012. We claim nothing about other seasons or cities, and the test weeks include the start of the summer holidays.
  • Settings fixed in advance. Model settings were not tuned per sensor; a search on the validation weeks might narrow the XGBoost / Random Forest difference.
  • Unknown readings are skipped, not filled. A live system would need a rule for sensors that go dark.
  • MAPE is dominated by low speeds and should be read alongside MAE.
  • The neighbour features depend on the published road map. Errors in the distance table would propagate.
  • No significance test. The two best models are 0.01 mph apart.
  • Speed only. METR-LA has no flow or occupancy; the PeMS source (Chen et al., 2001) does, and is the natural extension.
  • No control experiment yet. Any claim about traffic-signal control waits for a simulator in Phase 2.

11. Next steps (Phase 2)

  1. Train a Graph Attention Network on the same road graph, with the same one-hour history, horizons, split and masked scoring, so the comparison with Phase 1 is exact.
  2. Check the attention against the physics: does the network learn to listen to downstream neighbours in rush hour, as the backward wave predicts? Explore an LWR-based penalty as the hybrid ML/physics component.
  3. Ablations on how many hops the network looks out, which adjacency it uses, and how much history it sees.
  4. Report the simple baselines (historical average, persistence, XGBoost spatial) next to the deep model, always.
  5. If time permits, a simulated multi-agent signal-control experiment in SUMO or CityFlow that uses the forecasts — reported strictly as a simulation.
